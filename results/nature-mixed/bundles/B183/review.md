## Final Meta-review

This paper introduces Universal Value-Function Uncertainties (UVU), a single-model method for estimating epistemic value-function uncertainty in reinforcement learning. UVU trains an online network via temporal difference (TD) learning on synthetic rewards generated by a fixed, randomly initialized target network. The squared prediction error between the online learner and the target network serves as the uncertainty estimate, providing policy-conditional and long-term value uncertainty without requiring ensembles. The paper provides a theoretical analysis using neural tangent kernel (NTK) theory, showing that in the infinite-width limit, UVU prediction errors are exactly equivalent to the variance of an ensemble of universal value functions. A finite-sample result proves that multi-headed UVU errors follow the same distribution as ensemble variances. Empirically, UVU is evaluated on an offline multi-task GoToDoor environment with a task-rejection protocol, achieving performance comparable to large ensembles while being computationally more efficient.

### Strengths

- Novel and elegant method that combines RND-style prediction errors with TD learning to estimate policy-conditional value uncertainty using a single model, avoiding the computational overhead of deep ensembles.
- Strong theoretical contribution: derivation of closed-form NTK solutions for semi-gradient TD learning dynamics (Theorem 1) and exact equivalence between UVU errors and ensemble variance in the infinite-width limit (Corollaries 1 and 2).
- Computational efficiency: UVU achieves performance comparable to large ensembles (e.g., 35 models) using a single model with multiple heads, offering substantial computational savings.
- Clear exposition with intuitive tabular chain MDP examples that build understanding before the formal theory.
- Authors are transparent about the idealizations in their theoretical analysis (infinite width, gradient flow, fixed dataset) and discuss the gap between theory and practice.
- Well-designed task-rejection protocol that directly tests the quality of uncertainty estimates.

### Weaknesses

- Empirical evaluation is limited to a single environment (GoToDoor) with a single task-rejection protocol, limiting the generalizability of the claims about practical effectiveness.
- No comparison with more modern or stronger single-model uncertainty methods (e.g., DEUP, distributional RL, Bayesian approximations, or NTK-based approaches) beyond RND variants.
- The gap between theoretical idealizations (infinite width, gradient flow, full-batch, fixed dataset/policy) and practical settings (finite width, minibatch SGD, target networks, evolving policies) is acknowledged but not quantitatively validated beyond a width ablation.
- The width ablation (Figure 4a) shows performance stability but does not directly verify the theoretical equivalence between UVU errors and ensemble variance in finite-width settings.
- Sensitivity to the number of heads M and the initialization of the target network is not analyzed in depth.
- The 'single-model' claim is somewhat overstated given the multi-headed architecture (e.g., 128 heads in some experiments), though heads share hidden layers.
- The theoretical analysis assumes a fixed dataset and offline policy evaluation; extension to online RL with changing data distributions is not addressed.

### Questions

- How sensitive is UVU's performance to the number of heads M? Is there a trade-off between computational cost and uncertainty estimation quality?
- Can you provide direct empirical evidence that finite-width UVU errors quantitatively approximate ensemble variance (e.g., comparing UVU errors vs. ensemble variances across widths)?
- How would UVU perform in an online RL setting with exploration, where the data distribution changes over time? Does the theoretical equivalence still hold approximately?
- In practice, target networks are used for bootstrapping instead of the stop-gradient operation analyzed in the theory. How does this architectural choice affect the theoretical guarantees? Have you compared the stop-gradient version with the target-network version empirically?
- What is the impact of the target network initialization (scale, distribution, architecture) on the quality of uncertainty estimates?
- Have you considered comparing UVU with other single-model uncertainty methods such as DEUP, distributional RL, or Bayesian neural networks?
- How does UVU perform on standard offline RL benchmarks (e.g., D4RL) or more complex continuous control tasks?
- In the task-rejection protocol, how is the rejection threshold determined? Is it fixed or tuned per environment?
- Could you provide more detailed computational cost analysis, including memory usage and FLOPs, beyond just runtime?
- How critical is the independent bootstrapping procedure for generating diverse estimates across heads? What happens if it is removed?

### Limitations

- Theoretical analysis relies on strong idealizations: infinite network width, gradient flow with infinitesimal step sizes, full-batch updates, and static datasets/policies. The extent to which these assumptions hold in practical RL settings is not thoroughly validated empirically.
- Empirical evaluation is restricted to a single environment (GoToDoor) and a single task-rejection protocol, which may not generalize to other RL problems or real-world decision-making scenarios.
- The method requires a policy encoding z, which may not be readily available in all settings. How to define and generate meaningful policy encodings remains an open question.
- The multi-headed architecture, while sharing hidden layers, still requires multiple output heads, increasing memory and compute compared to a truly single-head model. The 'single-model' claim should be carefully qualified.
- The assumption of positive definiteness of the matrix (ΘXX − γΘX'X) in the TD learning dynamics is not guaranteed in general and may fail for some MDPs, potentially limiting the applicability of the theoretical results.
- The paper does not discuss potential negative societal impacts explicitly, though the topic is generally benign. The authors could briefly discuss risks of overconfident uncertainty estimates in safety-critical applications.
