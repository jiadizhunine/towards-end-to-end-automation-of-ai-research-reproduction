## Final Meta-review

The paper introduces XModBench, a large-scale tri-modal multiple-choice benchmark for evaluating cross-modal consistency in omni-modal large language models (OLLMs). It contains 60,828 questions derived from 10,138 unique instances, each instantiated across all six context/candidate modality pairs among text, vision, and audio. The benchmark spans five task families (perception, spatial, temporal, linguistic, and external knowledge) and 17 subtasks. Experiments on 13 closed- and open-source OLLMs plus a human baseline reveal that current models struggle with spatial/temporal reasoning, exhibit significant audio deficits, and show directional asymmetries, indicating they are far from modality-invariant. The proposed diagnostic metrics (modality disparity and directional imbalance) provide interpretable summaries of these weaknesses.

### Strengths

- The benchmark is the first to systematically cover all six cross-modal direction pairs among text, vision, and audio, enabling fine-grained diagnosis of cross-modal consistency.
- The scale and breadth are substantial: 60,828 questions, five task families, 17 subtasks, and a diverse set of data sources including human evaluation.
- The introduced metrics (modality disparity and directional imbalance) go beyond average accuracy and offer actionable insights into model weaknesses (e.g., audio being the weakest modality).
- The evaluation spans many state-of-the-art open- and closed-source OLLMs, providing useful reference numbers for the community.
- The failure-case analysis illustrates concrete cross-modal reasoning asymmetries and adds practical value.

### Weaknesses

- The evaluation protocol for non-text candidates is critically underspecified: how audio or visual choices are presented to models, the exact prompts, and answer extraction are not described, severely hindering reproducibility.
- The central claim of 'cross-modal consistency' is not directly measured: only aggregate accuracy differences across configurations are reported, not per-instance prediction agreement or pairwise consistency.
- The semantic equivalence of content across modalities is not rigorously guaranteed; text descriptions, images, and audio may carry different information granularity (e.g., a movie poster vs. a plot summary), confounding modality comparisons.
- No statistical significance testing or confidence intervals are provided, so it is unclear whether observed differences are systematic or due to sampling noise.
- Potential training-data contamination is not addressed, especially for web-collected data (IMDb, YouTube, GTZAN, etc.) and for questions generated by LLMs that are also evaluated.
- The human evaluation is limited to 10 participants per configuration without inter-annotator agreement or detailed recruitment/instruction descriptions, weakening its validity as a ceiling reference.
- There are internal inconsistencies and presentational errors (e.g., abstract's temporal accuracy vs. Table 2, truncated/malformed tables), and some metrics (modality disparity) lack principled justification.
- The exclusion of GPT-series models due to API limitations narrows the external validity of the findings.

### Questions

- What is the exact input format and prompt for each of the six modality configurations, especially when candidates are audio or visual? How are answer choices A-D associated with non-text candidates, and how are model responses parsed?
- Can the authors provide per-instance cross-modal consistency metrics, such as the fraction of instances answered correctly across all six configurations or pairwise agreement rates, rather than only aggregate accuracy gaps?
- How was semantic equivalence across modalities verified during data creation? What is the inter-annotator agreement, and how were instances with potential information asymmetry handled?
- Were any statistical significance tests or bootstrap confidence intervals computed for model comparisons and human baselines?
- What precautions were taken to prevent data contamination, particularly for web-collected data and LLM-generated content, and how would contamination affect the external-knowledge and other results?
- Why do the reported numbers differ between the abstract and Table 2 (e.g., temporal reasoning for Gemini 2.5 Pro)? Which values are correct?
- In the dual-context experiment, Gemini 2.0 Flash degrades when audio is added; why is this interpreted as a gain overall?
- What are the exact per-subtask dataset statistics, and how are the distractors balanced across modality configurations?

### Limitations

- The evaluation setup is not reproducible due to missing details about prompts, non-text candidate presentation, and output parsing.
- The benchmark does not directly quantify per-instance cross-modal consistency, so conclusions about 'modality-invariant reasoning' are based on accuracy gaps rather than prediction agreement.
- Semantic equivalence across modalities is imperfectly controlled; some tasks rely on different information sources (e.g., poster vs. plot summary), making 'consistency' ill-defined.
- The use of web-scraped data (trailers, posters, songs) and synthetic TTS raises potential copyright and privacy issues, and licensing details are not provided.
- The benchmark is multiple-choice only and may not capture open-ended cross-modal reasoning or real-world interaction.
- Data contamination from public datasets and LLM-generation is not assessed, which may inflate absolute scores.
- The evaluation omits important models (e.g., GPT-4o) due to API limitations, limiting generalizability.
- Statistical rigor is lacking: no confidence intervals, significance tests, or repeated run variances.
