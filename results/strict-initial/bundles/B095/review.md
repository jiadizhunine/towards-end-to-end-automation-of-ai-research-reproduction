## Final Meta-review

The paper introduces Feedback Forensics, an open-source toolkit for measuring AI personality traits in pairwise model response data. It uses AI annotators to select which response better exhibits a given trait, then compares these trait annotations against human preference labels (to measure traits encouraged by feedback datasets) or against target-model labels (to measure traits exhibited by specific models). The toolkit computes metrics including relevance, Cohen's kappa, and a combined 'strength' metric. It is demonstrated on human feedback datasets (Chatbot Arena, MultiPref, PRISM) and on several models, including a case study comparing two versions of Llama-4-Maverick. The authors release the toolkit, a web app, and annotation data.

### Strengths

- Addresses a timely and important gap: explicit measurement of AI personality traits, motivated by real-world incidents such as model rollbacks and leaderboard overfitting.
- Provides a practical open-source toolkit with a Python API, Gradio app, and web platform, lowering the barrier for researchers and practitioners.
- The strength metric combining relevance and Cohen's kappa is intuitive and interpretable.
- Demonstrates the toolkit across multiple real-world datasets and models, revealing domain-specific and model-specific differences, including a concrete Llama-4-Maverick case study.
- Releases the toolkit, web app, and annotation data under open licenses, supporting reproducibility and community reuse.
- Includes a human validation study (albeit limited) providing some evidence for the reliability of AI personality annotations.

### Weaknesses

- Human validation is extremely limited: annotations from a single author, only 100 comparisons and 10 traits, which is insufficient to establish the reliability or generalizability of AI annotators.
- The manually curated set of 40 personality traits is subjective and not systematically justified; it includes capability-like traits (e.g., 'factually correct', 'strictly follows requested output format') that conflict with the paper's own definition of personality.
- The strength metric and reported results lack uncertainty quantification (confidence intervals) and statistical significance testing, making it unclear whether observed differences are meaningful.
- AI annotator biases are acknowledged but not deeply investigated; the AI judge may itself favor verbose or well-formatted responses, potentially inflating apparent human preferences for such traits.
- No systematic comparison to existing personality evaluation approaches (e.g., psychometric methods, ICAI, VibeCheck) is provided, making it difficult to assess the toolkit's added value.
- The model comparison uses only 500 prompts and a single reference model (GPT-4o), limiting generalizability.
- The toolkit is demonstrated on static datasets; the claimed 'tracking' of personality changes over time is not demonstrated.

### Questions

- How robust are the strength values to different data subsets and repeated AI annotator runs? Are the observed differences statistically significant, and can confidence intervals be computed?
- How were the 40 traits finalized, and how stable are the results if a different trait set (e.g., generated by ICAI) or an established taxonomy (e.g., Big Five) is used?
- How does the choice of AI annotator backbone (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Flash vs. others) affect the results? Have robustness checks with different annotators been performed?
- How are ties (both/neither) handled in Cohen's kappa and strength computation, and could that bias the metrics?
- Could the observed personality differences be confounded by prompt distribution, decoding parameters, or annotator stylistic preferences? How are these controlled for?
- Does the web app support longitudinal tracking of personality drift over time or only static analysis?
- What safeguards exist against AI annotator position bias beyond the randomized-order kappa variant?

### Limitations

- All measurements are relative to the underlying prompt/response distribution and reference models; results may not generalize across domains, languages, or time periods.
- The reliance on LLM-as-a-judge introduces inevitable biases, and the limited human validation does not sufficiently mitigate this concern.
- The trait list is subjective and potentially incomplete, and may miss culturally specific personality dimensions.
- The framework is correlational; it does not establish causation between human preferences and personality traits.
- The release includes annotation data but not the original prompt/response content, which may limit reproducibility for some researchers.
- Potential negative societal impacts include misuse for manipulating model personality or profiling users; the paper mentions but does not deeply address these risks.
