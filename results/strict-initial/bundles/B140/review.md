## Final Meta-review

The paper presents Lunguage, a benchmark dataset for structured radiology report generation supporting both single-report and longitudinal patient-level evaluation. It includes 1,473 expert-annotated chest X-ray reports and 80 sequentially annotated reports from 10 patients, with fine-grained entity-relation-attribute annotations and EntityGroup/TemporalGroup structures. The authors also propose a two-stage LLM-based structuring framework that extracts schema-aligned triplets and performs temporal grouping, and introduce LunguageScore, an interpretable metric that compares structured outputs across semantic, temporal, and structural dimensions with partial credit. Experiments validate the structuring framework (F1 0.94 for entity-relation, 0.86 for full triplets) and the metric on ReXVal (correlation -0.58/-0.69 Kendall/Pearson), and benchmark several report generation models in single and sequential settings.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important and under-explored problem: longitudinal evaluation of radiology report generation, moving beyond single-report analysis.
- The benchmark dataset is carefully constructed with expert radiologist involvement, fine-grained schema, and detailed longitudinal annotations (EntityGroups and TemporalGroups).
- The proposed two-stage structuring framework achieves strong performance on entity-relation and triplet extraction while providing a viable automatic structuring mechanism for silver-standard evaluation.
- LunguageScore is a novel, interpretable metric that combines semantic, temporal, and structural dimensions with partial credit, offering fine-grained diagnostic feedback.
- The experimental setup includes validation on ReXVal and benchmarking of multiple state-of-the-art generation models, providing a useful performance comparison.

### Weaknesses

- The sequential dataset is very small (10 patients, 80 reports), limiting statistical power and generalizability of findings.
- No inter-annotator agreement metrics are reported for the sequential annotations, raising concerns about reliability of EntityGroup/TemporalGroup labels.
- LunguageScore does not outperform existing LLM-based metrics (GREEN, FineRadScore) on ReXVal, and its advantage over simpler metrics is modest; the claim of clinical superiority is not strongly supported.
- The metric relies heavily on the structuring framework; errors in entity grouping (F1 ~0.68) could propagate and affect the reliability of sequential evaluation.
- The sequential sensitivity analysis shows very small effect rates (0.2–0.9% per flipped attribute), suggesting that the temporal component may have minimal impact on the final score, undermining the metric's ability to detect temporal incoherence.
- The gold-standard annotations were initially generated by GPT-4 and then revised by radiologists, which could introduce LLM bias into the benchmark.
- The exclusive use of MIMIC-CXR chest X-ray reports limits the generalizability of the schema and metric to other modalities or datasets.

### Questions

- How was inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Cohen's kappa) for EntityGroup and TemporalGroup annotations assessed? What were the values and how do they support annotation reliability?
- Given that LunguageScore underperforms GREEN and FineRadScore on ReXVal, what are the practical benefits of your metric over these LLM-based metrics that were explicitly designed to align with the ReXVal error taxonomy?
- How sensitive is LunguageScore to errors in the automatic structuring framework, especially in the sequential setting with EntityGroup F1 of 0.68? Would using gold-standard structures versus automatically extracted structures yield substantially different model rankings?
- The sequential sensitivity analysis reports very small per-attribute effect rates (e.g., 0.2-0.9%). Does this indicate that the temporal component contributes negligibly to the overall score? If so, how does this align with the claimed emphasis on temporal coherence?
- What matching threshold is used to determine whether a predicted finding is matched to a gold finding? Since even low similarity contributes partially to TP/FP, how does the metric avoid overcounting or undercounting when findings are very different?
- Were any safeguards implemented to ensure that GPT-4's initial annotation suggestions did not bias the radiologists' final revised annotations, given that the same LLM is later used in the structuring framework and metric development?

### Limitations

- The sequential dataset is limited to 10 patients, which may not capture the full variability of longitudinal radiology trajectories.
- There is no cross-validation or inter-rater reliability analysis for the sequential annotations.
- LunguageScore is validated on a single-report benchmark (ReXVal) only; the sequential version lacks direct validation against human judgments of temporal coherence.
- The framework and metric depend on LLMs (e.g., GPT-4), which may introduce hallucination and variability; although vocabulary matching mitigates this, it may not be generalizable.
- The benchmark covers only chest X-ray reports from MIMIC-CXR, limiting applicability to other imaging modalities or clinical settings.
- The paper does not provide an error analysis of the structuring framework's impact on the final metric scores, particularly for sequential evaluation.
