## Final Meta-review

The paper presents a streaming, token-level hallucination detection method for LLMs. It frames hallucination detection as token labeling of entity spans in long-form generation, using an LLM-with-web-search annotation pipeline to create training labels. The authors introduce LongFact++, a large prompt set, and train lightweight linear or LoRA probes on hidden states to predict hallucination tokens in real time. Experiments across five model families show AUC around 0.87-0.90, outperforming uncertainty-based baselines, with cross-model transfer, KL regularization to preserve model behavior, and a selective-answering proof-of-concept. The datasets are released to facilitate reuse.

### Strengths

- Novel streamable token-level entity-focused detection that enables real-time intervention without external verification.
- LongFact++ and the annotated token-level datasets are valuable community resources.
- Consistent and often large improvements over uncertainty baselines across multiple model families, including held-out HealthBench and cross-model generalization.
- KL-regularized LoRA training offers a tunable trade-off between detection accuracy and preservation of model behavior.
- Extensive label-quality audits (human agreement, synthetic error injection, cross-annotator robustness) and low training cost support reproducibility.

### Weaknesses

- The automated annotation pipeline has significant noise: 80.6% recall on synthetic hallucinations, a 15.8% false positive rate, and only 84% human agreement on n=50 spans; this noise is not accounted for in reported AUCs and may inflate gains over baselines.
- There is a circularity risk: evaluation labels are generated by the same LLM-with-web-search pipeline used for training, with no independent human-verified long-form test set.
- No comparison is made against external verification methods such as SAFE or FactScore, or against semantic entropy probes, limiting the claim of practical advantage in accuracy or latency.
- The entity-level scope only captures fabricated entities; claim-level errors, reasoning mistakes, and context-dependent hallucinations are not addressed, and the MATH generalization result is not mechanistically explained.
- Selective answering sacrifices a large fraction of correct answers (e.g., attempt rate drops from 76.1% to 19.1% for Llama-3.3-70B), and long-form R@0.1 is only around 0.7, indicating that the method is not yet deployment-ready.
- The method requires white-box access to hidden states, limiting applicability to API-only or closed models.

### Questions

- How do the token-level probes compare to external verification methods like SAFE or FactScore in terms of entity-level precision/recall, end-to-end latency, and cost on the same test sets?
- How does label noise from the annotation pipeline (80.6% recall, 15.8% FPR) affect the reported AUC and reliability metrics, and can a larger human-annotated long-form test set be released to break the circularity?
- Is the MATH generalization robust when using step-level labels instead of a single max-token score, and does the probe primarily capture general uncertainty rather than entity fabrication?
- What are the exact hyperparameters (LoRA rank, alpha, layer fraction, alpha/omega schedules) and how sensitive are the main results to these choices?
- In cross-model experiments, are the training and test prompts disjoint across models, and could shared prompt sources inflate transfer results?
- How does the probe perform across different entity types (names, dates, citations), and does the span-max loss encourage over-broad activation?

### Limitations

- Automated annotation noise and the use of the same annotator for training and evaluation create a performance ceiling and potential bias.
- Entity-level detection is a narrow operationalization of hallucination, missing reasoning errors, relational errors, and source-grounded inconsistencies.
- The selective-answering proof-of-concept demonstrates low absolute reliability and significant drops in attempt rate, making it unsuitable for high-stakes deployment.
- The approach relies on a proprietary frontier LLM with web search for annotation, which may not be accessible to all researchers, and requires white-box model access.
- Generalization to domains beyond LongFact++, non-English text, and non-instruction-tuned models remains unexplored.
